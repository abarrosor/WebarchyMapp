# WebarchyMapp — user guide

A personal R/RStudio project that downloads official open data about recorded crime and police activity — 47 countries of Europe and the Americas by their official territories, 17 cities by neighbourhood — loads it into a local DuckDB database and shows it in a Shiny app with maps, in 32 languages. Everything runs on your computer; nothing is sent anywhere and nothing costs money.

## 1. What the app shows

| View | Data | Level | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Country › Spain** (the app opens here) | Known criminal offences by region, province and municipality (> 20,000 inhabitants); in Catalonia also by police area (Mossos, a second source of the country) | Quarter / year | Ministry of the Interior (Crime Balance); Government of Catalonia |
| **Country › 46 others** | Recorded offences (or complaints, cases...) by the levels each country publishes, from its official source; the About page lists them all | Quarter / year | National statistics offices, ministries, police forces, Eurostat |
| **Cities › Madrid** | Calls received by the Municipal Police by neighbourhood; police activity by district; arrests by motive (city) | Month | Madrid City Council (datos.madrid.es) |
| **Cities › Barcelona** | Incidents handled by the City Police by neighbourhood; recorded crime by district (Mossos) | Month | Barcelona City Council (Open Data BCN); Mossos |
| **Cities › Lima and Callao** | Police complaints by distrito (the 50 distritos of Lima Metropolitana and the Callao) | Month | Ministry of the Interior of Peru (SIDPOL) |
| **Cities › Paris, Lyon, Marseille** | Recorded crime by arrondissement, 15 SSMSI indicators (yearly figures: the source publishes no months) | Year | Ministère de l'Intérieur, SSMSI (data.gouv.fr) |
| **Cities › Amsterdam, Rotterdam, Den Haag, Utrecht, Eindhoven** | Crimes registered by the police by CBS wijk (Amsterdam also by stadsdeel); any other gemeente is one line | Month | Politie / CBS (table 47022NED) |
| **Cities › London** | Offences recorded by the Metropolitan Police by ward and borough (the City of London is not included); rates from the ONS mid-2022 ward estimates | Month | Greater London Authority, London Datastore; ONS |
| **Cities › Mexico City** | Investigation files (carpetas de investigación) of the prosecution service by colonia and alcaldía | Month | Fiscalía General de Justicia de la CDMX (datos.cdmx.gob.mx) |
| **Cities › Berlin** | The Kriminalitätsatlas by Bezirksregion (143) and Bezirk (12): seven offence groups, residents implied by its frequency figures | Year | Polizei Berlin (daten.berlin.de) |
| **Cities › Buenos Aires** | Reported offences by barrio (48) and comuna (15); rates by comuna and city from the INDEC census of 2022 (the barrios have no official population) | Month | Buenos Aires Data (Ministerio de Justicia y Seguridad); INDEC |
| **Cities › Zürich** | Offences of the Swiss Criminal Code by Stadtkreis (12), by title of the code, with the residents of each Kreis | Year | Kantonspolizei Zürich, open data of the Kanton Zürich |
| **Cities › Toronto** | Reported major crimes by neighbourhood (158), nine categories, with the portal's population estimates | Year | Toronto Police Service, Public Safety Data Portal |

Every figure is shown with its source, what it measures and its limitations. Different sources are **never added together or compared with each other**: a call is not a crime, and Madrid's categories are not Barcelona's.

## 2. First time

1. Install R (4.3 or newer) and RStudio.
2. Open `WebarchyMapp.Rproj` in RStudio.
3. Open `WEBARCHYMAPP.R` and press **Source** (Ctrl + Shift + S). A menu appears in the console.
4. Choose **5** to install the R packages (once).
5. Choose **1** to download and load everything. The first run takes hours (47 countries and 17 cities; Canada alone parses 50 GB, Mexico City's file is hundreds of MB) and a portal may be down that day: the run continues with the next source and says what failed. To go in parts: option **13** (countries), **8** (cities) or **14** with a list of codes. Downloads are not re-done on later runs; a country whose files did not change is not reloaded.
6. Choose **2** to open the app (English) or **3** to pick another language.

## 4. Using the app

**Light or dark (the button at the top left)**
- The page opens in the look of your system: light or dark. The button at the top left of every page turns it round (a moon to go dark, a sun to go light).
- **Nothing of your computer is read or kept by the project.** The question is answered by the browser of whoever opens the app, at that moment (the standard question `prefers-color-scheme`); the code holds no setting of anybody's computer. A choice that differs from the system's is remembered by that browser, for this app alone; a choice that is the system's own is not kept, so the page follows the system again.
- **In the dark look a map is a dark map.** One rule exchanges light and dark for the whole map and keeps its hues: its tiles, its areas, its legend, its zoom buttons, its labels. The areas and their legend can never disagree, because the same rule turns both: the group with most is the strongest colour in both looks, the dark blue of the light map and the bright blue of the dark one. That is why the help texts of the maps say *a stronger colour does not mean danger* and no longer *darker*. The charts are drawn again in light ink on the dark ground; the colours that carry figures (the blue of the bars, the paler bar of an incomplete period) are the same in both looks.

**Moving between places (the top bar)**
- Two menus: the **countries** (its title is the country of the page) and the **cities**. Each is a list in alphabetical order in the page's language that keeps inside the window and scrolls, with the place of the page marked; the cities show their country beside them.
- With more than 8 places the menu has a **box to type in**: the list narrows as you type (accents and capitals do not matter), the best match is marked and **Enter** opens it; the arrow keys walk the list and **Esc** closes it. A place is found by its name in any of the 32 languages, by its code and by other usual names: `deutschland` or `germany` find Alemania, `reino unido` finds England and Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland, `holanda` the Netherlands, `la haya` Den Haag; typing a country in the cities menu lists its cities.
- They are ordinary links: a middle click or Ctrl + click opens the place in a new tab.

**Tables: any column gives the order**
- **Click the title of a column** to put a table in the order of that column, and click it again to turn the order round. The ▼ / ▲ mark shows the column and the way in use. Numbers start from the largest, names from A. With the keyboard: Tab to the title, then Enter or the space bar.
- **A row without a figure stays at the end** whichever way the column is ordered (a count under 10, a rate that cannot be worked out): where it stands says nothing about a figure that is not shown. Equal figures go by name. A total keeps the foot of its table.
- It holds for every table: the bordering areas of a profile, the types of a neighbourhood's profile, a city's ranking, the panels of a city (persons detained by motive; a district against its city), the breakdown of a territory by type of offence, the three tables of *About the data*, and the *Table* tab of a country.
- **The order you choose stays** while you move (another neighbourhood, another period, a row clicked), until you change it or load the page again. One exception, a city's **ranking**: which areas are listed is decided by the control above it (*Sort by*: rate or number) **or by a click on the title of the column of numbers or of rates** (the title of the ratio to the city goes as the rate), whichever was touched last; the control moves with the title. The other titles, and a second click on the title the ranking already goes by, order the rows on screen, and a ranking drawn again starts in the order of the control.
- Two tables have nothing to put in order and no titles to click: the three bars of the rates of a profile (neighbourhood, district, city) and an indicator of a survey (the district and the city, year by year).

**Cities**
- *Filters*: category (public safety / nuisance; the blocks each source allows), type of call, incident or offence (only those with events in the period are listed, with counts), period slider with *This year · 5 years · All* views (years instead of months for the yearly cities: Paris, Lyon, Marseille, Berlin, Zürich, Toronto; only the views that differ are offered: *5 years* when the series is longer than that, *All* when it has more than its latest year, and none at all for a series that fits in one year, as Madrid's), shading by rate (default) or number.
- *Map*: hover a neighbourhood for a summary; click it (or search it) to open its **profile** in a **card over the map**, at the right, with a cross to close it (Esc closes it too) while the filters stay in view at the left. *Show in the side panel*, at the top of the card, puts the profile back in the side panel (the filters then fold to give it room) and *Show over the map* brings it back; the browser remembers the choice. On a narrow window or a phone the card is a sheet from the bottom. The profile holds: events, rate per 1,000 residents, comparison with the district and the city (location quotient and percentile band), the **bordering areas** (the neighbourhoods whose outline touches its outline or comes within 60 metres of it: the same types and period, the neighbourhood marked among them from the highest to the lowest rate, the range and the median of the others above the table; click a name to open it), monthly bars or yearly evolution, types, and — in Madrid — the Municipal Police activity in the district; in Barcelona, the recorded crime of the district (Mossos) and the victimisation survey.
- *Map* keeps the sidebar (search box, filters, the profile of the neighbourhood). *Statistics* has no sidebar: a **bar across the top** holds the area (the whole city, a district or a neighbourhood; type a name to find it), the category, the types (a dropdown with the boxes and *all / none*) and the period. The filters are the same ones in both tabs — what is chosen in one is chosen in the other — and the area follows the neighbourhood picked on the map (and the map follows it: back on the map, its profile is open). Below the bar, **side by side on a screen at least 1,200 pixels wide** (one under the other on a narrower one): at the left the **chart by type** of the ticked types in that area and period, from most to least (figures under 10 shown as "<10"; a name too long for its chart is cut with "…"), and under it the *time of year*; at the right the **time series** with the rate of each complete year under it. Click a bar of the chart by type and the time series shows that type alone, click it again for all of them. Then, across the page, the top 5 (or 10) districts for the current filters, with the city average; in Madrid, people arrested or investigated by motive (whole city). Cities with one level (Rotterdam, Zürich...) show the statistics by neighbourhood.
- *Where you are*: the small box at the top left of the map names the city, the district and the neighbourhood open, with the level you are at marked; the city is the way back to the whole city.
- *On a phone*: a bar at the bottom switches between Map and Statistics and opens the menu; two month selectors replace the period slider; the profile opens as a sheet over the lower part of the map.
- *A yellow note under the category* (and inside the profile) means that the source lacks some types in the chosen period: it names the years, how many of the category's types are missing and the three largest. Missing is not zero: such a period cannot be compared with the others. Today it shows in **Barcelona for 2024 and 2025**: the two yearly files the city publishes are cut at 30,000 lines and stop in the middle of the *fights* code, so public safety has no data in those years (nuisance is whole). A category left without any type shows the note and no figure, and the page opens on the first category that has events. If the portal publishes the files whole, the next update removes the note by itself.
- *Time series* (at the right of the chart by type; under it on a narrow screen): bars of the selected types for the selected neighbourhood (or the whole city), **monthly, quarterly or yearly** at your choice. It opens by quarter; a series of up to two years of months (Madrid's calls begin in January 2026) opens by month, and the links to the last years are offered only when the series is longer than they are. However few the bars are, each keeps a moderate width instead of filling the screen. Under the chart, the **period bar**: a small chart of the whole series with **two handles — where the period starts and where it ends — and a label above each one**; the part outside them is faded. Drag a handle to move that end, drag the window between them to move the whole period, or press anywhere else on the bar and the nearest handle comes to the pointer (keyboard: Tab to a handle, then the arrow keys; Home and End go to the ends). The handles move by whole bars of the chart (months, quarters or years), and the period stays when the grouping changes (a quarter chosen stays whole when seen by month). The *last year · 3 years · 5 years · everything* links do the same in one click. Click a bar to read its value. An incomplete last period is lighter and marked *; figures under 10 are not drawn. Under the bars, as many names as fit side by side, counted back from the latest period, which is always named. Yearly cities (Paris, Berlin...) show years only. **Per 1,000 residents, full years**, under the chart by number and above the period bar, which both charts share (as in the countries): the rate of each **complete** year for the same area and types — the events of the year, with the "fewer than 10" rule, on the residents of that year —, with the city as a dashed line when the area is a district or a neighbourhood. It shows the complete years that the period of the bar touches; a line gives the residents of the first and the last year in view and which way they went; a † marks a year whose residents are those of another year (a city with one register has it on every year but one); a year in which the source lacks some of the types has no rate and a sentence says so (Barcelona's public safety in 2024 and 2025). A city without a register has no such chart, and a series that has no complete year yet shows its title and one sentence in its place: **Madrid, until December 2026 is published** (second half of January 2027). Under Madrid's charts a sentence says why the series begins in January 2026 — the City Council publishes these calls from that month on — and where the app keeps what exists for earlier years, which are other sources and are never added to this one. The **seasonal profile** (*time of year*: the average of each calendar month) stands under the chart by type once the city has a complete calendar year of months; until then, and in the yearly cities, the page simply has no such block.

**Country menu (Spain and the other countries)**
- The top bar has a *Country* menu with every country that has data loaded (Spain always; the others after option 1 or 13). Each country is a view of its own, with the levels its source publishes (for instance France: régions, départements, communes; Germany: Länder and Kreise; Ireland: Garda divisions only). The controls below are the same for every country, and **no figure of one country is ever compared with another**: definitions and recording rules differ.
- **No table of countries.** Until 17 November 2026 the top bar had *Countries (table)*: Eurostat's national rates per 100,000 inhabitants, the one place that put several countries side by side. It was taken out (P-063): it did what the rest of the app refuses to do, gave the national level only and covered Europe alone. The update no longer downloads that table; the copies already downloaded stay in `data/raw/shared/eurostat/`. Eurostat's own data browser shows those figures (`crim_off_cat`).
- A country whose source has no boundaries file (or whose download failed) shows tables instead of a map.
- *Where you are*: the small box at the top left of the map lists the country and each of its levels with the territory you are in at that level, and marks the level the map shows. Click a row to go to that level (the same as the *Level* control): it is where a change of level made by zooming is seen. Going up a level keeps you in the same branch (the province of the municipality that was open).
- *Bordering areas* (in the profile of a territory, under its figures): the territories of the same level that share a border with it, of this country only, with the same source, offence types and period — the territory marked among them, from the highest to the lowest rate, and over the table the range and the median of the others. They are found from the outlines of the map: two territories border when their outlines touch or come within 60 metres of each other, whatever the level (the update simplifies the outlines of some countries, which pulls apart neighbours that share a short border; the 60 metres bring them back, and add now and then two territories that face each other across a square). So a country shown without a map has no such block, an island has no bordering area, and two territories that face each other across a strait or a wide estuary are not neighbours. A bordering territory the source gives no figure for (a municipality under 20,000 inhabitants in Spain) is counted under the table, not listed. Click a name to open that territory. Neighbours share streets, transport and visitors: similar figures are the usual thing, and a clear difference is a reason to look further, not a verdict.
- *Table* (second tab): the territories of the level, **from the highest to the lowest ratio against the reference** (the "× Spain" or "× region" column) when the page opens. **Click the title of a column to order by it** (name, inhabitants, number, rate or ratio) and click it again to turn the order round; the ▼ / ▲ mark shows the order in use. The country (and the region, at municipal level) stay on the first rows, and territories without a value go last.
- *Series* (third tab next to Map and Table): two charts for the selected territory (or the country) and the ticked offence types, one under the other, and **one period bar under both, which they share**. **By number**: the same bars as in the cities, **quarterly or yearly** (the sources publish quarters at most; yearly sources show years only). **Per 1,000 inhabitants, full years**, in the same style: the rate of each complete year on the population of that year, with the reference (the country or the region) as a dashed line with points. Both show the period chosen on the bar: the chart by number its quarters or years, the chart by rate **the complete years that period touches** (2021 Q3 to 2023 Q2 shows 2021, 2022 and 2023; the latest year alone, while it is incomplete, shows none). A click on a bar writes its value on the line under its own chart (for the rate: the territory, its reference, and its inhabitants against the year before). Over the chart by rate, a line gives **the inhabitants of the first and the last year in view and which way they went** ("3,223,334 (2018) → 3,506,730 (2025) ▲ +8.8 %"): a rate also moves when its people do. The chart by rate is shown when the country has population figures. England and Wales and Scotland count in financial years (April–March): their years read "2025/26" and a partial year runs from April.

**Spain**
- *Level*: regions, provinces or municipalities. With **Follow the map** (on by default) the level follows the zoom (all of Spain → regions; a few provinces → provinces; closer → municipalities), the municipalities of every region in view are shown (only those above 20,000 inhabitants, which are the ones with data), and the territory list and the table only contain what is inside the view. Untick it to pick a region by hand; in Catalonia the *Source* control then lets you switch to the Mossos police areas.
- *Type of offence* lists every offence type with figures in the period, ordered by count. With every type ticked the figure is the Ministry's official **total** (which also includes the sensitive types never listed: homicides, sexual offences, kidnapping...); with a subset it is the sum of the ticked types. *All · None* and clicking a type in the breakdown table change the selection.
- *Period* works like the city slider: *Latest year · 5 years · All* chips and a two-handle slider over the years. One handle position shows one year (the latest, partial one is listed on its own); a range **adds up full years** and the rates become **yearly averages**, so that they stay comparable with single years. The partial year is left out of any range.
- Each profile has a **rate comparison** card: how the rate compares with the reference (Spain, the region or the city) in five steps from "much lower" to "much higher" (ratio below 0.6, 0.6–0.85, 0.85–1.15, 1.15–1.5, above 1.5), the % difference and the position among the territories of the same level. It compares recorded figures per resident; it is not a safety or danger score. Click a territory on the map or in the table to open its profile: offences, rate, ratio to the reference (Spain or the region) and breakdown by type; the evolution of its rate year by year is in the *Series* tab.

**Cite this data** (a quiet link at the foot of the side panel of every city and country, and at the end of a city's statistics page): the citation of the **original source** — publisher, dataset, address, licence, the period on screen, the day the source was last read — with one sentence on what was done to the figures (aggregated by WebarchyMapp, a personal project that is not an official product of the publisher; counts below 10 not shown), a button to copy it and the same as BibTeX. The licences of the sources ask for exactly that when their data is reused. The app is never given as the publisher of the data: it distributes none.

**Download these figures (Excel)** (a button at the top right of a city's statistics page, under the bar): an Excel file with what the page shows at that moment for the area, the types and the period chosen, so that anyone can draw the figures their own way. Its first sheet, *About this file*, gives the choices of the page, the moment the file was made, what the figures are, the citation of the original source (the lines of *Cite this data*) and what each of the other sheets holds; then one sheet per chart: *By type* (every ticked type with a figure in the period; the chart draws the first twelve), *Time series* (the bars in view, in the unit the chart is seen in, each with its first day as a date), *Per 1,000 res.* (the rate of each complete year in view, the city's beside it for a district or a neighbourhood, and the residents), *Time of year* (the average of each calendar month) and the ranking (the rows the page lists). A chart that has nothing to show has no sheet. The rules are those of the page: a figure is a number, so that it can be drawn; one under 10 is an **empty cell with a note beside it**, never the count; a period the source lacks is empty too, never a zero; an incomplete period says so. The file does not hold every district and neighbourhood (that is the map, not this page), nor the panels of other sources, nor any country's page. It needs the free package `writexl` (option 5 installs it; without it the page has no such button). The columns open narrow, as in any exported file: widen them in Excel (select all, double-click the edge of a column).

**About the data** (link in the top bar; four tabs: sources, how each source counts, glossary, rules): what each source counts, its territory, the period loaded and its licence; the rules the figures follow (no mixing, threshold, sensitive types, no day or hour, partial years); why there is not more data (municipalities under 20,000, no crime by neighbourhood, Mossos and Ertzaintza inside the Ministry's totals, convictions and surveys, citizen contributions); and what the figures cannot say. The periods and dates come from the tables, so the page updates itself.

**Reading the figures**
- Figures below 10 are shown as "<10" and the area gets a dashed outline on the map. No day, hour or sensitive types (homicides, sexual offences, gender-based violence...) are ever shown by area; they only count in totals.
- Rates divide by registered residents: areas with lots of visitors (centre, nightlife, transport, tourism, airports) get high rates that overstate the risk for residents. The app warns when a rate is twice the reference.
- Darker on the map never means "dangerous": it means more calls, incidents or recorded offences per resident (or in total, if shading by number).
- **The population behind a rate is that of its own year.** The figures of 2024 are divided by the inhabitants of 2024 and those of 2025 by the inhabitants of 2025. A range of years is divided by the average of the population of each of its years (in a city, each year counts as many months as the period has in it), so that the rate of a range stays the yearly average. Countries that count by financial year (England and Wales, Scotland) take the population of the calendar year in which the financial year begins.
- **The inhabitants and their year are written where the rate is**: in the profile ("3,506,730 inhabitants (2025)", "average 2021–2025" for a range), in the title of the *Inhabitants* column of the table, in the note under the page and, in a city, under the title of the rate comparison ("26,000 residents (register 2024-01-01)") and under the statistics. When the period spans several years, the same line says which way they went: "26,000 (2024) → 26,500 (2025) ▲ +1.9%" (▲ up, ▼ down, = under 0.05 % either way).
- **A † means a rate stands on the population of another year**, because the year of the figures has none published yet (the latest months of a year whose register is not out) or never had one loaded (a city with one register only). The nearest earlier year is taken (the first there is, when none is earlier), and a note names both: "† No population figure for 2026: the rate uses the nearest published one (2025)." In the table of a country a † beside one row means that territory alone lacks the figure the others have. Option 15 lists the same thing for every place (`population_years_behind`, `population_starts_later`, `years_without_register`).
- **Which cities have a register for each year**: those whose source publishes one (Zürich, Toronto, Berlin, Paris, Lyon, Marseille, Lima; Barcelona, whose yearly files are downloaded from the first year of its incidents). Madrid publishes one register a month and only the current one: each year keeps the first register downloaded in it. Its calls exist since January 2026 only, so every period of the calls has the register of its year; the † shows in the police activity by district, whose months go back to 2014 and stand on the register of 2026. Cities with a single census or edition (Buenos Aires, London, Mexico City, the Dutch cities) are marked † outside its year.

## 5. Monthly routine

Madrid publishes each month in the second half of the following month; Barcelona once a year (around February); the Ministry every quarter; Mossos monthly; the Dutch police monthly; London monthly; Buenos Aires once a year; most national sources yearly or quarterly (the About page gives each one's period). Run option **1** whenever you want, or schedule it with option **9**. Then open the app (the tables it reads are rebuilt at the end of every update).

## 5b. What an update says at its end

Every update (options 1, 7, 8, 13, 14 and the automatic one) ends with a **summary of the run**: the things that did not go as planned, collected from the hundreds of lines above it and filed under the place that raised them.

EXAMPLE (the places and numbers are made up):

```
-- Summary of the run: what did not go as planned ------------------
NEW in this run: 2 line(s)
  Mexico City (CMX)
    download  Download not available (CMX): Timeout was reached [datos.cdmx.gob.mx]
  Poland (PL)
    warning   NAs introduced by coercion [in pl_ingest(con)] (3 times)
No longer happening: 1 line(s) of the last run of these places
  Costa Rica (CR)
    warning   One or more parsing issues ... (since 2026-11-03)
The same as in earlier runs: 41 line(s) in 17 place(s), the oldest since 2026-11-03
  Germany (DE): 14; Argentina (AR): 7; Chile (CL): 3; ...
Kept from earlier runs, not looked at in this one: 12 line(s) (steps that did not run: files unchanged, ...)
Every line, with the date it was first seen: reports/run_20261122_210512.csv
```

- **NEW** is what to read: it was not there the last time that place ran. **No longer happening** confirms that a fix worked, or that a portal is back. **The same as in earlier runs** is what you already know (Germany's addresses, a population file that is missing…): one count per place; the full list is in the CSV.
- The **first** summary on a computer lists everything once, in full: it is the reference for the next ones.
- The kinds: `failed` (a step raised an error: that part of the data stays as it was), `download` (a portal did not give a file: the copy on disk is used), `warning` (an R warning, with the call that raised it and how many times it came; each different one is also printed once where it happens), `unmapped` (the source has a category or a place the reference table does not know: section 6), `attention` (a line the module itself marks with `[!!]`), `stale` (below), `other` (a file that could not be read or understood).
- **`[old]` in the status lines** (and `stale` in the summary, `last_period_old` in option 15): the last period of that source ended longer ago than its rhythm allows — more than 8 months for figures by month or quarter, more than 20 for yearly figures, 36 for the Eurostat regional table. The update "works" and nothing new arrives: the address changed, the source stopped, or the file must be fetched by hand. If the alarm is false for a source (it really publishes that late), tell Claude: the module states its own rhythm in one line.
- The summary remembers by place: a run of two countries says nothing about the others, a run without downloads nothing about downloads, and a country kept because its files did not change keeps what its last load said. Those lines are not repeated: one number says how many were **kept from earlier runs, not looked at in this one**. The memory is `reports/run_state.csv`; deleting it only makes the next summary a "first" one again.
- The summary never gets in the way of the update. A line it cannot read (a name that a file gave in another encoding) is printed as it always was. If `reports/run_state.csv` is open in a spreadsheet when the run ends (Excel locks the files it has open), the summary is still printed and a line starting with `(Not saved:` names the file: close it, nothing else is needed; the next run compares itself with the last summary that was saved.
- The automatic update writes the same in its log, and its last line is the result (option 11 shows it; option 4 too).
- When R or a package changes version, the run says which (`reports/versions_<date>.csv` keeps the last set that was in use).
- A module's line reaches the summary by its wording: `[!!]`, a line starting with `x `, "not downloaded", "not available", "not in reference/….csv", "not readable"… (the list is `journal_kind()` in `R/05_journal.R`, with its test). A new module only has to keep to it.

## 5c. Two tools beside the routine

**Old copies (option 17).** A file that a portal republishes whole is saved again under a new date whenever its content changes, and the older copies stay: the project never deletes a download. The option lists the copies that a newer one replaced, by folder, with their sizes, and says which **can be moved**: only those that have a newer copy both by the date in the name and by the time of the file, and that no row of the database comes from (a series built from several copies keeps them all). To free room, move those files to another disk by hand, keeping the folder names, with the `.txt` listing in front; move, do not delete, so that a later rebuild (option 12) that missed one could have it back.

**Madrid's historical register (option 18).** The register dataset the project reads is one snapshot a month, kept from October 2026 on. The calls exist since 2026 and have their register; the police activity by district goes back to 2014, and its months before 2026 stand on the register of 2026 (marked †). The option reads the list of the historical dataset, proposes one file per year (the first month of each), asks before downloading and loads them. It was written without seeing that dataset (the portal cannot be read from where Claude works); the founder's first run, on 16 November 2026, sent back the portal's list, which is now read correctly: one file per month from January 2014, the month in the description of each ("Padrón municipal. Histórico. 2024. Diciembre"), so the option proposes the January file of each year from 2014 to 2025. **The old files themselves have not been read yet**, so it stays strict: a file is loaded only if it has the columns of today's register, a date that is known, between 2.8 and 3.8 million residents, and neighbourhoods that are today's; a district whose neighbourhoods were others in that year (Vicálvaro and Villa de Vallecas before 2017, as far as is known) is left out for that year and keeps its †. Whatever it refuses is said with its reason and appears in the summary. Send Claude what it prints the first time it downloads and loads them.

## 8. Adding a country

Each country, Spain included (`R/71_es.R`), is one R file (`R/7x_<cc>.R`, `R/8x_<cc>.R`, `R/9x_<cc>.R`) that registers itself with `register_country()` and one reference table `reference/<cc>_offence_types.csv` (source category -> offence type, English and local names, parent aggregate, sensitive flag). The file provides four functions: `download()`, `territory(con)`, `ingest(con)` and `population(con)`; the framework in `R/70_countries.R` does the rest (shared tables, marts, status). A country may carry a **second source** (a regional police, say): a file loaded after the country's that calls `register_source(cc, list(id, name, levels, level_labels, covers, ...))` with the same four functions and its own `reference/<cc>_<id>_offence_types.csv`; the app shows it as a switch of source inside the territory it covers (`covers` = a region id or the country code) and never adds it to the first one. `R/72_es_mossos.R` is the model. Use `R/71_fr.R` (France) as the model and `reference/eurostat_regions_countries.csv` to add a European country that only has Eurostat regional data. Rules: official sources with an open licence, no registration, raw files never modified, sensitive offences only inside totals, and never a comparison between countries.

A full run (options 1 and 13) does not parse a country again when nothing of it changed since its last load: the files of its folder `data/raw/country_<cc>/` and its reference tables are compared with the date of that load, and the log says "files unchanged since the last load, kept". A module that reads files kept in a shared folder names them with `watch` (a function that returns their paths: the Eurostat tables that 23 countries read, `R/92_eurostat_regions.R`), so that a new copy of one of them reloads the countries that read it. A change in the *code* of a module is applied with option 14 and the country's code.

Countries whose module could not be tested against the real files (the first download happens on your computer) print a clear message naming the file and its header when the structure differs from what was documented; run `<cc>_inspect()` (e.g. `fr_inspect()`) to see the headers and adjust the column patterns at the top of the module.

## 8b. Adding a city

A city is one R file (`R/6x_city_<key>.R`) that registers itself with `register_city()` (code, key, municipality id, names, language, country, source, and the four functions `download()`, `territory(con)`, `ingest(con)`, `population(con)`). The territory step writes the districts and neighbourhoods into `dim_territory` and `data/territory_<key>.gpkg` (layer `territory`); the ingest step calls `publish_city_events()` with a view of monthly events by official neighbourhood and a type table (`type, block, sensitive, name_es, name_en`); the population step fills `ref_population` with the city register by neighbourhood. The framework (`R/60_cities.R`) builds `mart_city_events`, `mart_city_event_types` and `mart_city_meta`, and the app shows the city with the same map, filters and profile as Madrid and Barcelona. City-specific texts go in `translations.csv` as `key@<key>` variants. A city may also carry a **survey** (victimisation, perception of security): `register_city_survey(key, list(id, name, publisher, url, licence, measure, ingest))`, whose `ingest(con)` calls `publish_city_survey()` with yearly values by city or district and the indicator definitions; the app shows it in the profile as a measure of its own (Barcelona's Enquesta de Victimització is the model, at the end of `R/62_city_barcelona.R`). Use `R/62_city_barcelona.R` as the model, `R/63_city_lima.R` for a city built on a country module's own data (it runs after the countries: `after_countries = TRUE`), `R/64_city_fr.R` for a **yearly source** and a **generator** (`fr_city_module()`: Paris, Lyon and Marseille from the SSMSI arrondissement rows; `time_unit = "year"` makes the app show years instead of months and no seasonal profile), `R/65_city_nl.R` for the Dutch generator (`register_city(nl_city_module(code, key, gm, name, id))`: one line per gemeente), `R/66_city_london.R`, `R/67_city_cdmx.R` and `R/69_city_buenosaires.R` for sources read from CSV files with their own portals, `R/68_city_berlin.R` for a yearly Excel workbook, `R/69a_city_zurich.R` for a yearly CSV that carries the residents, `R/69b_city_toronto.R` for an ArcGIS layer read in pages (counts, rates and polygons in one file). Module fields that change the texts: `measure_kind` ("calls", "incidents", "complaints", "offences", "cases") picks the set of texts the app uses when a key has no `key@<city>` variant (`key@offences`...), and `boundaries_attribution` is the credit under the map when `translations.csv` has no `boundaries_source@<key>`. A city may also carry **panels** (a second monthly dataset by district or for the whole city: police activity, arrests by motive, a regional police by district): `register_city_panel(key, list(id, kind = "district" | "city", name, publisher, url, licence, measure, ingest, ...))`, whose `ingest(con)` calls `publish_city_panel()` with monthly values by territory and indicator plus the indicator table; the app shows a "district" panel in the neighbourhood profile and a "city" panel in the statistics tab, with the translation keys the panel names or generic texts. The panels of Madrid (`R/61_city_madrid.R`) and Barcelona (`R/62_city_barcelona.R`) are the models.

Every city module prints a clear message when a downloaded file does not match what it expects (a portal changes a column, a sheet, a layer): `nl_city_inspect("rotterdam")`, `london_inspect()`, `cdmx_inspect()`, `berlin_inspect()`, `buenosaires_inspect()`, `zurich_inspect()` and `toronto_inspect()` show what was downloaded and the headers; the URLs and dataset ids live in `reference/<key>_sources.csv`, the type equivalences in `reference/<key>_event_types.csv`. Population comes with the source or from the statistics office named in the module (London: ONS ward estimates; Buenos Aires: the INDEC census by comuna; Berlin: implied by the atlas's frequency figures; Toronto and Zürich: in the file). Mexico City's colonias have no official population: rates exist for the alcaldías and the city only; its portal may not answer from some networks (then the two files by hand, as the message says).

## 9. Languages

The app has **32 languages** (the selector of the top bar; menu option 3): English, Spanish, Catalan and Galician; at least one official language of every country with data — Albanian, Bulgarian, Croatian, Czech, Danish, Dutch, Estonian, Finnish, French, German, Greek, Hungarian, Icelandic, Italian, Latvian, Lithuanian, Norwegian (bokmål), Polish, Portuguese, Romanian, Serbian, Slovak, Slovenian, Swedish and Turkish; and Chinese, Japanese and Korean. Montenegro has no column of its own: its constitution names Montenegrin as the official language and puts Serbian, Croatian and Albanian "in official use", and those three are there.

**Who wrote them.** Every translation was written by the assistant, not by a native speaker. The tests check what can be checked without one (placeholders, HTML, one word per concept, names quoted exactly), not the style. The least certain languages are Icelandic, Albanian, Estonian, Latvian, Lithuanian and Slovenian. A wrong word is corrected in its cell of `app/translations.csv` (an editor that keeps UTF-8), and option 16 says at once if a placeholder was broken.

**What is translated and what is not.**
- *The texts of the app* (`app/translations.csv`, 690 keys): all of them in the 32 languages, with one word per concept in each language (rate, level, indicator, period, type, the two categories, the unit each source counts).
- *The names of the offence categories*: a country page shows the names of its source in the language of the source, and English in every other language. The exception are the 15 names all the countries share (the Eurostat / ICCS categories, `app/category_names.csv`, in the 32 languages): the *Countries* table and the countries that only have the Eurostat figures are translated. A list is translated whole or not at all — never half of it.
- *Left in English* outside the language of the source: the national category names (about 1,650 names, some 1,250 different ones, in 36 reference tables), the method notes and source descriptions of each module, and the names of some levels (those without a `lvl_` row).

**How a text is built.** A number followed by a noun goes through the `n_noun` row and a noun followed by "per 1,000 inhabitants" through `noun_rate`, so that each language puts them in its order (many put the noun first: "asukkaat: 5 000"); no sentence relies on the form a noun takes after a number. A row named `key@city` or `key@kind` is the variant of a text for one city or one kind of source, and `lvl_<level>@<country>` the name of a level in one country (Peru's *departamentos*); a variant must be filled in every language, because an empty cell shows the English of the variant.

**Adding a language.** Add a column to `app/translations.csv` with the language code as header and translate the texts (keep the `%s`/`%d` placeholders and the `|`-separated month lists), the same column to `app/category_names.csv`, then add the code and name to `LANGUAGES` in `app/i18n.R` and to the list of option 3 in `WEBARCHYMAPP.R`. Empty cells fall back to English. Names of call types, offence types and arrest motives come from the reference CSVs (the source's language and English only). Three families of rows feed the menus of places: `country_<CODE>` (the name of a country), `cityname_<key>` (the usual name of a city in each language: Londres, La Haya, 伦敦; without the row the menu shows the name of the city's territory) and `placewords_<CODE>` (other words that find a place when typed: "Reino Unido", "Holanda", "Czech Republic"; optional).

## 10. Privacy and interpretation rules built into the project

1. Never day or hour: month × neighbourhood at most.
2. Sensitive types are never shown by area.
3. Only blocks that can be read by area (public safety, nuisance); traffic, ambulance support, 092 closures and the like are left out.
4. Figures below 10 are suppressed after adding up the chosen months.
5. Totals never mix blocks, measures or sources.
6. No rankings of neighbourhoods; the district top 5 always shows rates, raw numbers and the city average, with a note on transient population.

## 11. Known problems

- **"LoadLibrary failure: Una directiva de Control de aplicaciones bloqueó este archivo"** (or the English "An Application Control policy has blocked this file") when a package loads, e.g. `classInt.dll`, `duckdb.dll`. Windows 11's Smart App Control checks the reputation of every DLL and blocks one it does not know; R packages are not signed, so a freshly updated package can be blocked while the rest load. It is the computer, not the code or the data. **First, simply try again**: the refusal can last a moment. On 2026-11-16 `duckdb.dll` was refused once at the start of the tests (one test failed, the one that opened the database first), loaded seconds later in the same run, and the next run passed in full; nothing had to be restarted or reinstalled. In an update the same thing shows as one failed step in the summary of the run (section 5b), and the rest goes on: run the update again. If the same file is refused every time: stop the app, type `install.packages("<the package named>")` in the console (straight quotes), restart R and try again: the current CRAN binary usually has reputation. Seen 2026-11-09 with `classInt`; solved that way. If the new file is blocked too, the options are turning Smart App Control off (Windows Security → App & browser control) or running R under WSL2. Turning it off is a decision about the security of the computer, not a step of this guide: this note first said it could not be undone; ❓ several headlines of 2026 say a Windows update now lets it be turned back on, which was not verified: read Microsoft's own page before deciding.

- **"Failed to connect to <portal> port 443 ... Could not connect to server"** (seen with datos.cdmx.gob.mx): the server never answered. The download layer tries once more over IPv4 only (a portal whose IPv6 address is dead fails in R while a browser falls back silently). If it still fails, open the portal in your browser: if the page loads, download the files by hand into the folder the message names (Mexico City: the cumulative carpetas CSV or zip into `data/raw/city_cdmx/carpetas/`, the colonias GeoJSON or shapefile zip into `data/raw/city_cdmx/colonias/`, then option 14 `CMX`); if the browser cannot load it either, the portal is down or blocks connections from abroad — try later or from another network.

## 13. Automated tests

A few minutes (most of the time goes into drawing every view in the 32 languages).

| | The working documents of the project (decisions, risks, plans): they live outside this folder |

`.gitignore` says so to git. **Menu option 19** says it a second time and looks inside every file: it does the `git add`, checks the whole set and, if one file fails (a data file, an e-mail address, the folder of a user, a drive path, a key or a password), unstages everything again and lists the reasons. It writes a report in `reports/git_prepare_<date>.txt` and, when there is none newer than the last commit, a draft of the message in `reports/commit_message.txt`. It never commits and never pushes. The same check runs on the project's files in the automated tests (option 16).

**Every time**, in a terminal opened in the project folder (RStudio: *Tools > Terminal > New Terminal*), after option 19 has said READY:

```
git status
git commit -F reports/commit_message.txt
git push
```

(In *git gui*: option 19 has already staged the files; write the message, *Commit*, *Push*.)

**The first time only.**

1. **Create the repository on github.com** (*New repository*): empty, without README, licence or .gitignore (the project has its own). Public or private is your choice.
2. **Decide which e-mail address signs your commits.** Whatever address a commit carries is public with it, for ever: a published history cannot be unpublished. GitHub gives every account a private address: *Settings > Emails > Keep my email addresses private* shows it (`NUMBER+USER@users.noreply.github.com`), and *Block command line pushes that expose my email* refuses a push that would publish the real one. To sign with it from now on (type your own number and user):
   ```
   git config user.email NUMBER+USER@users.noreply.github.com
   ```
   Option 19 says who would sign the next commit (the address in part) and which addresses the history already carries. It reads your own git settings the way git gui and a terminal do. If it says that no e-mail is set, git will refuse to commit: set your name and address in git gui (*Edit > Options*), where your earlier commits took them from.
3. **Decide what to do with the commits already made**, which carry the address they were made with:
   - *Publish them as they are*: that address becomes public.
   - *Publish a history that starts today* and keep the old one on this computer only. After step 2 and option 19:
     ```
     git checkout --orphan public
     git commit -F reports/commit_message.txt
     git remote add origin https://github.com/USER/REPOSITORY.git
     git push -u origin public:main
     ```
     The branch `master`, with the old commits, stays here and is never pushed (push `public` only).
   - *Keep the history and change its signature*: every commit is rewritten with the new address. It takes a command that cannot be typed without quotes, or the tool `git filter-repo`: ask for a menu option if this is the choice.
4. If you publish the history as it is: `git remote add origin https://github.com/USER/REPOSITORY.git` and then `git push -u origin master`.

**If option 19 says that git add failed with *Permission denied***: on Windows another program (an antivirus that reads every new file, a search index, a sync client, git gui) can hold a new file open for a moment, and git gives up. Option 19 tries again by itself, up to eight times, and says how many tries it took; nothing is lost between tries. If it still fails, close git gui and any other git tool and run it again.

**If option 19 says NOT READY**: nothing was staged and no file was changed. A file that must stay out goes into `.gitignore`; a line that names an address, a folder or a key is rewritten; then option 19 again. A web address of a source, the folder where Windows installs programs and a GitHub private address are not problems and are not reported.
